# Raw-feature baselines: what does the physics feature engineering buy?

Generated by `evaluation/prediction/scripts/external_baselines_report.py`
from the canonical tables, so every number here matches
`stratified_metrics.csv` exactly.

## The comparison

Every arm in the main table shares this system's engineered feature set --
Morinaga Md, lattice mismatch, gamma-prime fraction, Labusch-Nabarro terms,
temperature derivatives, grain-boundary and refractory aggregates, 50-80
features depending on target. The baselines below use **raw inputs only**:
25 element weight percents plus the test temperature,
26 features, nothing derived.

Protocol is identical in every other respect -- GroupShuffleSplit(0.15,
random_state=42) by alloy for the holdout, GroupKFold(5) for CV,
inverse-square-root sample weights, and the same Optuna budget the
production models received (30 trials, TPESampler seeded at 42, negative
R2 objective). The GPR follows the published Ni-superalloy methodology
(Conduit et al., *Materials & Design* 131 (2017) 358-365): standardised
composition and temperature, anisotropic Matern kernel at nu = 2.5 with a
white-noise term, marginal likelihood optimised from restarts, fixed
rather than tuned.

## What this shows

| property | ML-only (engineered) | best raw-feature baseline | difference |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| YS | 101.16 | RF raw 95.01 | -6.1% |
| UTS | 112.96 | RF raw 111.33 | -1.4% |
| EL | 13.84 | GPR raw 12.66 | -8.5% |
| EM | 12.86 | GBM raw 14.87 | +15.6% |

**On strength and ductility the engineered features do not pay for
themselves.** A RandomForest on eleven raw numbers matches or beats the
engineered-feature ensemble on yield strength, tensile strength and
elongation. On yield strength it is also close to ML+physics+KG, which
adds knowledge-graph retrieval on top of the features.

**On elastic modulus they do pay.** ML-only at
12.86 GPa beats every raw baseline, and the deterministic
Voigt-Reuss-Hill layer on top of it reaches
8.21 GPa, which no purely learned arm here
approaches. Modulus is the property with an actual closed-form physical
estimator, and that is exactly where the physics contributes.

### The stratum profile is the more interesting result

| property | arm | NEAR | FAR |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| UTS | ML-only | 96.55 | 138.41 |
| UTS | RF raw | 135.42 | 113.78 |
| YS | ML-only | 107.56 | 115.02 |
| YS | RF raw | 105.62 | 109.96 |

The engineered features are better on NEAR and worse on FAR; the raw
features are the reverse. For tensile strength the gap is large in both
directions -- ML-only is 40 MPa better on the near-duplicate alloys and 25
MPa worse on the compositionally distant ones. That is the signature of
features that encode the training set's regime rather than transferable
physics, and it should be reported: the feature engineering appears to buy
recall of familiar alloys at some cost to generalisation.

### What is unaffected

None of this touches the agent-layer result. The full system reaches
80.89 MPa on yield strength against the best raw baseline's
95.01 and the best engineered ablation's
94.83, so its contribution is measured
against a stronger floor than the paper previously established, and
survives.

## Limits

- The production models were tuned once, months earlier, on the same
  budget but not in this run; the baselines were tuned fresh here. If
  anything that favours the baselines slightly, and the comparison is
  reported in that direction rather than against it.
- Raw features exclude the processing route, which the engineered set
  carries as a categorical. That handicaps the baselines on properties
  where wrought and cast differ sharply, and they still match on three of
  four.
- 77 training alloys is small enough that a RandomForest on eleven inputs
  is not obviously under-parameterised relative to 80 engineered features.
  The result may say as much about dataset size as about feature quality.
